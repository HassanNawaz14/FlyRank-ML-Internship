Which SEO Signals Actually Predict Content Decline?

I tested whether pre-decline signals — content staleness, click-through rate relative to search position, and word count — actually predict which pages will lose search visibility, using 111,247 real content pages from FlyRank's search-performance warehouse. A simple, readable rule (stale content + weak click-through rate) held up better under honest testing than a more complex model that looked stronger under a naive one. The result is a validated, precision-led review queue that a content team can trust and act on, with its limits stated plainly rather than discovered later.

01 — Introduction / Problem

A reviewer can't check every page. Something has to decide what gets checked first.

A content or SEO reviewer at a company managing thousands of pages cannot personally re-read every page every month to catch decline early. They need a short, trustworthy list: which pages are worth a look this week, and why. Get that list wrong in one direction and a reviewer wastes time on pages that were never actually declining. Get it wrong in the other direction and a real decline goes unnoticed until it's expensive to reverse.

FlyRank's own March 2026 research paper, The State of AI-Driven SEO, found across 341,701 pages that refresh timing is "one of the strongest measured levers available" — refreshed 365+ day content showed a 3.2× health boost and 57× more impressions than untouched pages of the same age. That finding motivates the question this study answers at the individual-page level: which signals, measurable before a decline happens, are actually associated with a page declining — and can a simple, explainable rule built on those signals be trusted, or does it need a heavier model to hold up?

02 — Data

111,247 pages, three real signals, one honest label.

This study runs on FlyRank's pseudonymized search-performance warehouse — daily Google Search Console metrics joined to content metadata across 104 clients and roughly 520,000 distinct content items. Every page in scope has at least 100 impressions in the 30 days prior to the analysis window, which brings the working set to 111,247 pages — 21.4% of the full portfolio. That floor is deliberate: below it, click and position metrics become too noisy to trust, but it means this study's findings apply to pages with meaningful existing traffic, not the long tail of near-zero-impression content.

Time windows used
WindowRole
prev30The 30 days used to build every feature — staleness, CTR, position, impressions
last30The most recent 30 days, used only to compute the label — never seen by any model as a feature

The label: a page is marked declining when its impressions drop more than 20% from the prev30 window to the last30 window. That threshold is stricter than FlyRank's own published paper, which uses a 10% cutoff — checked directly against this dataset, the two definitions disagree on 6,563 pages (5.9% of the working set), a reminder that "decline" is a modeling choice, not a fixed fact.

Excluded — direct leakage imp_last30, clk_last30, pos_last30, trend_direction, trend_pct — every one of these falls inside the exact window used to define the label. A correlation check against the label confirms why: these columns correlate at −0.14 to +0.18, meaningfully higher than any legitimate pre-decline feature.
Excluded — window overlap risk Query-level signals from the 90-day query table (rare_share, top_query_share) were deliberately never used. That table's fixed 90-day window overlaps the label period with no way to split it into safe and unsafe parts, so it was excluded entirely rather than used with an asterisk.
03 — Methodology

A rule you can read, a model that has to beat it, and a split that can't lie.

The baseline: two signals, plainly combined

Before any model, a simple rule: flag a page when it has gone 90+ days without an update, or when its click-through rate sits below the median for pages at its search-position tier — whichever positive-median tier it belongs to. Two tiers, page_3_5 and deep, have a median CTR of exactly zero, which would make "below median" impossible to trigger; a page there is instead flagged weak-CTR only if it earns literally zero clicks. Each page gets a reason code:

STALE+WEAK_CTR STALE WEAK_CTR NONE

The models

Two models were trained on the same pre-decline features (prior-window impressions, clicks, position, CTR, staleness, word count, content type, intent): Logistic Regression, chosen because its coefficients are directly readable — this study's whole purpose is finding out which signals to trust, not just getting the highest score — and Random Forest with permutation importance, as a nonlinear cross-check on whether a different method agrees on what matters.

The split that mattered most

Pages from the same client tend to share templates and publishing habits. A plain random 80/20 split lets the model see near-duplicate patterns from the same client in both training and testing — which makes a model look better than it actually is at handling a genuinely new client. Every real result in this paper uses GroupShuffleSplit on client ID instead, confirmed to produce zero client overlap between train and test. Section 04 shows exactly how much the naive version would have misled this study.

Reading this table honestly No single approach wins outright. The baseline rule is the most precise — when it flags a page, it's right most often, which matters for a queue reviewers must trust repeatedly. Logistic Regression catches the most true declines (highest recall and F1) but at the cost of more false alarms. Random Forest, despite being the most complex model, is the weakest of the three on the metric that matters here.

Why the split, not the model, was the real test

ModelRandom split (naive)Grouped split (honest)Inflation

Under the naive split, Random Forest would have been reported as the best model in this study — it out-scored Logistic Regression by nine points of F1. Under the split that actually respects how this model would be used (scoring pages from clients it has never seen), that advantage reverses entirely. Random Forest's higher capacity let it quietly memorize client-specific patterns that a random split allowed to leak between training and testing; Logistic Regression, being simpler, had far less capacity to do the same, which is exactly why its score barely moved.

What the two models actually leaned on — and where they disagree

Only one signal was robustly confirmed by both methods: ctr_prev30 was Logistic Regression's single largest coefficient and Random Forest's third-highest permutation importance. Past that, the two disagree — Random Forest ranked days_since_last_update and word_count as its top two signals, while Logistic Regression assigned both near-zero weight. That gap is most likely explained by an honest limitation, not a real contradiction: the numeric features were never scaled before fitting Logistic Regression, so its coefficient magnitudes aren't fairly comparable across features of very different natural scale. This is disclosed here rather than smoothed over, per Section 05.

What the errors looked like, concretely False positives — flagged as declining but weren't — mostly shared one trait: zero clicks despite being reasonably fresh (29–41 days since update). A CTR of exactly zero is enough to trigger a flag even when staleness doesn't support it. False negatives — missed real declines — mostly had a decent CTR (0.3–1.0%) and sat right around the 90-day threshold, suggesting some real declines happen for reasons neither signal in this study captures.
05 — Limitations & honest framing

What this study can say, and what it deliberately won't.

  • The label is a proxy, not a proven outcomeDeclining means impressions already dropped more than 20% in the most recent 30 days — a description of the current window, not a guaranteed future trajectory for any individual page.
  • Coverage stops at 21.4% of the portfolioThe 100-impression floor exists to keep CTR and position metrics trustworthy, but it means these findings say nothing reliable about the long tail of low-traffic pages.
  • Modest discriminative powerAUC-ROC of 0.55–0.60 sits closer to chance than to strong separation. This is a prioritization aid for a time-constrained reviewer, not a confident prediction.
  • Logistic Regression's feature ranking is scale-confoundedUnscaled numeric features mean its coefficient magnitudes are directional evidence at best — a fully fair comparison against Random Forest's importances would need a re-fit on standardized features.
  • Observational, not causal, throughoutEvery relationship reported here is an association measured in this portfolio. None of it proves that updating a page, or its click-through rate, causes a change in performance.
  • A global time anchor, not a per-client oneAll windows are anchored to one snapshot date across every client; clients whose data ends earlier are proportionally underrepresented.
06 — Ranked recommendations

The queue a reviewer actually gets, and what should never be automated.

The baseline's reason code drives the primary ranking — not the model — because it is the most precise and the most explainable of the three approaches tested. The model's output is used only as a secondary, within-group confidence signal.

Refresh, highest prioritySTALE+WEAK_CTR

90+ days without an update and getting fewer clicks than similar pages at its position. Two independent warning signs — 8,930 pages.

RefreshSTALE

90+ days without an update; click performance still looks normal for its position — 6,684 pages.

MonitorWEAK_CTR

Reasonably fresh, but fewer clicks than similar pages at its position — worth a snippet or intent-match check — 50,144 pages.

LeaveNONE

No warning signs from either check — lowest priority, 45,489 pages.

Never automate

  • No auto-editing, auto-redirecting, or auto-unpublishing content based on this score alone.
  • No using this score as a judgment of a writer's or team's performance.
  • No applying this playbook to clients or pages outside the coverage confirmed in Section 02 — it was never validated on them.
  • No treating a flagged page as confirmed to be declining without a human check for context this model can't see — evergreen intent, inherently low-CTR traffic, or a recently deleted/unpublished page.
